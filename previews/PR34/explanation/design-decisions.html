Design decisions ​

The decisions that shape FFTL, each with the reason and the evidence it rests on. A decision stays here until something replaces it; a replaced decision is marked, so a reader who finds the old reasoning elsewhere can see that it no longer holds. Numbers are measurements on an Apple M1 Max and on AMD EPYC (Zen 2) unless a line says otherwise.

Licence and provenance ​

Decision. FFTL is MIT-licensed. No GPL code is read, copied, translated or closely paraphrased. FFTW is a benchmark competitor only. Concepts known from the literature are used; code structure, naming and style deliberately do not follow FFTW (a 25-year-old C library with generated codelets and a measuring planner). Permissive sources may be read; a close take-over keeps its notice. The rules for contributors are on the Contribute page and in AGENTS.md.

Codelets with complex values in SIMD registers ​

Decision. A codelet works on complex values interleaved in SIMD registers. On aarch64 with the ARMv8.3 complex-number extension it uses FCADD and FCMLA, elsewhere shuffles (x86: vfmaddsub for products, addsub for ±i). Whether the extension is present is decided when the package is compiled: Apple silicon always, Linux by the fcma flag in /proc/cpuinfo (LLVM's host feature query returns nothing on macOS).

Why. On the M1 this layout with FCADD/FCMLA was the first variant that beat FFTW; keeping split real and imaginary parts in registers spilled for Float64. The layout is a per-platform choice behind dispatch on the vector type, so the generator and the plan structure stay shared.

A heuristic radix choice, no measuring planner ​

Decision. The radix sequence of a length comes from a fixed rule on its prime factorisation (src/factor.jl). There is no measuring planner by default.

Why. Brute-force searches over all decompositions put the best and the second-best within 1–3 %, and the large losses of an estimate-only planner are cache effects the algorithm has to avoid anyway. A per-machine calibration or an optional measuring mode can follow if the rule leaves too much on the table.

One such cache effect is part of the rule: single lines on AVX-512 take radix 16 only while the 16 streams of a stage are less than an L1 set stride (4 KB) apart, that is up to 4096 for Float32 and 2048 for Float64. Beyond that the streams share one cache set and radix 8 wins. AMD EPYC 9354, one line, all buffers at fixed page offsets, time of the radix-16 sequence over the radix-8 sequence: Float32 4096: 0.88, 8192: 1.06, 16384: 1.06; Float64 4096: 1.33, 8192: 1.03, 16384: 1.11.

Rules for lengths are derived on some lengths and checked on others ​

Decision (2026-11-04). The radix choice and the Bluestein length are rules on the prime factorisation, not tables per length. A rule is derived from one part of the lengths (a brute-force search over all decompositions, or a cost model) and checked on lengths that were not used for the derivation, for example by parity of n or by a random split; only the checked result counts as evidence. A mechanism with a stated cause (a cost estimate) is preferred over a list of lengths. Rules are per architecture where the search differs. After every change of a rule, the dense sweep is repeated and its distribution (median, share above 1.2×, 1.5×, 2×, worst lengths) is the criterion, not single points.

Why. The first rules were fitted to a few dozen lengths, mostly powers of two and smooth lengths on the M1. A dense sweep of every length 2…1024 against FFTW MEASURE (one thread, Zen 2, 2046 cases) showed 100 Float32 and 64 Float64 lengths slower than 1.5×, the worst at 3.4×.

Lengths with large prime factors: split off the smooth part, Bluestein on the rest ​

Decision (2026-11-07). For n = s·q, with q the part made of primes above MAX_RADIX_PRIME (73, at the time of the decision 31): one Cooley–Tukey step, a chain of length s, twiddles, and s Bluestein transforms of length q (Composite). A leftover q stays one Bluestein while its convolution is at most 4 MiB; above that, q is split into its prime powers, recursively. Bluestein convolutions of 8 MiB and more run as a serial four-step algorithm on x86 (1.3× from 2²⁰ on, Zen 2) but not on aarch64, where the chain is faster up to 2²² (M1 Max). With threads, CompositeMT is used when s is at least the number of threads and is bitwise equal to the serial kernel; otherwise the whole length is one BluesteinMT.

Why. The convolutions of length about 2q stay in cache, and the arithmetic equals one Bluestein of length n. On 45 non-smooth lengths (Zen 2) FFTL moved from ×0.64 to ×1.04 of the fastest other library with one thread, and from ×0.77 to ×1.16 with 16 threads.

Not done / not checked. Rader's algorithm (5 of the 45 sweep lengths qualify). Both thresholds were measured on Zen 2 only, and the 4 MiB rule is checked on 12 lengths that also took part in its derivation.

One chain form: the radix sequence is data ​

Decision (2026-11-13). Every smooth length runs on RChain: the radix sequence and the stage records are data, the codelets come from a fixed set. There is no chain type with the radix sequence in its type, and nothing is compiled per length. RChain is also the only form the trimmed C library can contain.

Why. The compiled chain (Chain{T,Rs,σ,K}, used for smooth lengths up to 128 until this decision) existed because the run-time chain cost 9–20 ns more per call on x86. That cost was not the choice of the codelet at run time (about 1 ns per stage) but the code around it: GC frames for references loaded from the chain, the copy of an immutable chain into each caller, bounds checks, and one more call level per stage. With those removed (see Implementation) the compiled chain has no advantage left. AMD EPYC 9354 (AVX-512), one thread, plan_fft and mul!, 92 cases (Float64 and Float32; lines of 2 to 4096, planes of 8 × 8 to 128 × 128, cubes of 8³ to 64³, in-place, backward and real transforms), 8 rounds in alternating processes, median time over the median time with the compiled chain:

  • geometric mean 0.973; 27 cases below 0.95, 8 above 1.05;

  • lines of one stage (2, 4, 16): 0.80–0.95; 8 × 8, 8 × 8 × 8, 7 × 12, 5 × 64: 0.85–0.91;

  • the cases above 1.05 are short lines of two or three stages: Float32 20, 32, 96 and 100 (1.09–1.10), 37 rows of 64 (1.13), rfft of 64 (1.07 and 1.09), and Float64 12 × 7 (1.05). The stage loop runs about 50 instructions per stage around the codelet (no mispredicted branches), 2.4 ns per stage against direct calls of the two kernels of Float32 32.

  • The machine was shared during the measurement: over the 8 rounds the slowest time of the compiled chain over its fastest is 1.04 in the median over the cases, 1.19 at the 90th percentile and 1.55 at most.

Not verified on aarch64.

What the single form removes: 583 compiled chain methods and their generators, the dispatch trees from an RChain of a short length into the compiled chains, and the compilation at the first plan of a short length with a radix sequence outside the compiled set (a short dimension with a large stride). The package has 1796 methods instead of 2385, the package image on this machine is 61 MB instead of 72 MB, and libfftl.so (x86-64-v3, --trim=safe) is 49.5 MB instead of 55.6 MB.

Replaced. The two chain forms (2026-11-09: compiled Chain for smooth lengths up to 128, RChain for the rest), and before that the idea of removing Chain without changing the stage loop (2026-11-08) and the separate JIT and run-time plan families (2026-11-02).

Kernels of a fixed set are plain methods, not @generated ​

Decision (2026-11-10, fallback removed 2026-11-13). The stage kernels of the codelet set that RChain dispatches to are plain methods, defined with @eval in src/kernel_methods.jl from the functions that build their bodies. One table (STAGE_KERNELS in src/kernels.jl) lists the kernels and their arguments; the methods and the dispatch switches of RChain are made from it. There are no @generated stage kernels: every combination the chain can select has a plain method, so a fallback would never run.

Why. A package image keeps the complete optimised IR of every precompiled @generated specialisation, 130 MB of the 146 MB image. See Package size and load time.

The first stage of a single line runs at full vector width ​

Decision (2026-11-12). Where the widest vector holds 8 complex values (Float32 on AVX-512), the first stage of a single line runs stage_small!: vectorised over the butterflies, with the outputs interleaved in registers into contiguous stores. It exists for the power-of-two radices and belongs to the fixed set of plain methods.

Why. The grouped first stage handles at most 4 complex values per vector. Intel Core i7-1185G7, one line, Float32, lengths 128–16384 with radices of 16s and 8s: 12–26 % faster (the same code against itself differs by up to 3 %).

Not done. The same for later stages with a stride of 2 or 4. It needs a per-lane twiddle table and one kernel per stride, and only Float32 lengths whose radices start with 4, 4 have such a stage. On an AMD EPYC 9354 it gained 6–8 % at 192 and 320 and nothing measurable at other lengths.

Stage kernels keep twiddles in memory and address streams from few registers ​

Decision (2026-11-12). In the stages vectorised over q, the twiddle multiply reads re(w) and im(w) from the table (ccmul_at; memory operands on x86), and the R input and R output streams are addressed as base + odd·stride·2^e from one base per 8 streams (stream_addr). The base pointer is hidden from LLVM's loop optimiser (opaque_ptr), which otherwise goes back to one pointer per stream.

Why. A radix-16 stage has 15 twiddles and 32 streams, more than fit in registers, so its inner loop reloaded vectors and pointers from the stack. AMD EPYC 9354 (AVX-512), instructions per butterfly: radix 16 middle stage 284 → 198, radix 8 100 → 79. One line, plan_fft, medians of 8 interleaved rounds: lengths 512–16384 take 0.79–0.94 (Float32) and 0.82–0.92 (Float64) of the time, lengths from 32768 on are unchanged (the same code against itself differs by 1–5 %). Not verified on aarch64.

The prime radices from 17 to 73 share one looped kernel ​

Decision (2026-11-13). A stage of a prime radix from 17 on runs prime_stage! (src/primestage.jl): one kernel for all such radices, with the radix and a table of cosines and sines as arguments, compiled once per vector type. It uses the symmetric form of the odd-prime codelet as loops and computes up to eight output pairs (four with 16 vector registers) in one pass over the inputs. It covers the stage kinds that the codelets have separate kernels for: stages vectorised over the lanes, the last stage, lane blocks, and the grouped first stage. MAX_RADIX_PRIME is 73. The radices up to 16 keep their straight-line codelets. The KernelAbstractions kernels and the generic number types keep their limit of 31.

Why. A straight-line codelet of a prime r has about r²/2 statements with as many different constants, which do not fit in registers (525 statements for 31), and every radix needed 82 kernel methods on AVX-512 (kind × sign × vector type). The codelets of 17–31 were therefore compiled at first use and not with the package (decision of 2026-11-10, replaced by this one). With the loop, a prime costs a table.

Measurements: AMD EPYC 9354 (AVX-512), one thread, plan_fft and mul!, rounds in alternating processes, median time over the median time of the previous code.

  • Lengths and shapes with a factor 17–31 against the codelets (40 cases, 4 rounds): geometric mean 0.82, 36 cases below 0.95, one above 1.05 (Float64 100 × 31, 1.06: a single stage in place, which copies the inputs of a butterfly first). 17 and 31 alone: 0.63–0.84; 1088 = 17·64: 0.96 and 0.89; 837 = 27·31 and 841 = 29²: 0.72–0.82; 62 × 62: 0.70–0.74.

  • Long lengths (36 cases, 3 rounds; 31·2⁶ to 17·2²⁰, and a factor 17–31 times a prime above 1000): geometric mean 0.94, one case above 1.05 (Float64 19·2¹⁷, 1.08). 31·2⁶ to 31·2¹⁴: 0.74–0.91; 17·2²⁰: 0.99 and 1.01.

  • The primes 37–73 against Bluestein's algorithm, which they used before (100 cases, 2 rounds; p, 8p, 64p, 1024p, 16384p): geometric mean 0.29. The prime alone: 0.43–0.79 (Float64) and 0.48–0.90 (Float32); as a factor of a longer length: 0.13–0.53.

  • 92 general cases without such factors (the set of the decision above, 4 rounds): geometric mean 1.00 without the two cases with a dimension of 37 (0.10 and 0.16), 3 cases above 1.05 (Float64 lines of 32, 48 and 60: 1.07–1.15). In 6 rounds on 20 short lines alone these three were at 1.04, 0.83 and 1.05 and the geometric mean 1.00; the stage kernels of these lengths compile to the same machine code as before.

  • Accuracy against a 200-bit reference, Float64, 841, 6256 and 6859: 1.4–1.8 ε, with the codelets 3.5–4.6 ε (the table is built from angles reduced to one turn).

What made the loop as fast as the unrolled sums: several rows per pass, so that the inputs are loaded once for all of them and the multiply-add chains of the rows run side by side; no buffer for the sums and differences of the inputs (storing whole registers was the slowest part: 1.3–1.4× per butterfly with the buffer); the pass over a block of rows out of line and the outputs of a block written by recursion over the tuples of accumulators (a loop with an index into them, or a closure over them, put the accumulators on the stack: up to 1.4× for a whole transform).

The limit. With the limit at 127 for the measurement, a prime from 79 on is still 1.3–4× faster than Bluestein's algorithm as a factor of a longer length, but the line of that prime alone is slower from 79 on for Float32 (1.05, 1.5 at 127) and from 97 on for Float64 (1.01, 1.15 at 127). 73 is the largest prime for which every measured case is faster.

Not done. The loop for 11 and 13: with one kernel per radix (an earlier form of the loop) it was at 0.98 of the codelets for Float64 and 1.05–1.06 for Float32, and 1.08–1.10 for 7; not measured with the single kernel. A larger limit for lengths that are not a prime alone. aarch64 is not verified.

Costs. The kernel and its tables are in the package image, which the lazy codelets were not: the image file is 66 MB instead of 59 MB on this machine (68.5 MB before the decision on the chain form). The C library contains every kernel in either case and shrinks: libfftl.so (x86-64-v3, --trim=safe) is 26.8 MB instead of 49.5 MB, its code 8.8 MB instead of 18.6 MB.

Run-time pass plans for trimmed programs ​

Decision (2026-11-10). A pass plan (plan_pass, plan_r2c_pass, plan_c2r_pass) built with _runtime (plan_pass_runtime, plan_r2c_pass_runtime, plan_c2r_pass_runtime) uses run-time kernels only: RChain, Bluestein and composite transforms over RChain, the four-step algorithm over RChain. Its type depends on the element type and the number of dimensions, not on the length. The specialised plans are unchanged.

Why. A library that holds passes of many lengths in one container (as NFFT.jl does for its boxes) has a container of an abstract or length-dependent type, and juliac --trim cannot resolve the calls through it. The first attempt was a keyword runtime = true; the verifier then still checks the specialised branch, whose kernel types depend on the length, so the separate functions were needed. A probe library compiled with juliac --trim=safe holds run-time pass plans in a vector, applies them, and matches the direct DFT to 3·10⁻¹⁴ when called from C. Planning on 64 × 64 × 64 views is 1.8 µs for d = 1 and 0.9 µs for d = 2 on the M1 (the specialised plan: 4.7 µs and 2.8 µs). Applying the specialised and the run-time plans is within noise of each other on the same views.

Costs. For real lengths with large prime factors, the run-time kernel is a Bluestein transform, without the composite split of the specialised plan.

The interface: FFTW.jl's names, FFTL's own functions, AbstractFFTs as a backend ​

Decision (2026-11-06). FFTL exports FFTW.jl's names (fft, bfft, ifft, rfft, brfft, irfft, the plan_ and in-place forms, the dct family, and the AbstractFFTs helpers), so using FFTL can take the place of using FFTW. r2r, the kinds, the flags and set_num_threads stay public with the prefix, as in FFTW.jl. The DFT and DCT functions are FFTL's own, not AbstractFFTs's; the package only does import AbstractFFTs, never using AbstractFFTs or import AbstractFFTs: fft. Generic code that calls AbstractFFTs.fft reaches FFTL through methods of plan_fft and its relatives for AbstractArrays of floating-point numbers (src/abstractffts.jl), which are less specific than FFTW.jl's StridedArray methods: with FFTW loaded, the generic functions take FFTW for its arrays and FFTL for the rest.

Why, and what was rejected.

  • Exported names that are AbstractFFTs's functions (re-exported bindings) made FFTL.fft === FFTW.fft: any dependency that loads FFTW would take the standard arrays away from code that wrote using FFTL, and the GPU extension's methods would have landed next to CUDA.jl's.

  • Methods with FFTW.jl's exact signatures: the package loaded last overwrites the other for the whole process, and a package depending on both fails to precompile ("Method overwriting is not permitted during Module precompilation").

  • Methods are global per process, so this is the only arrangement in which a scope with using FFTL reaches FFTL whatever a dependency loads. Tested: with B -> FFTL and B -> A -> FFTW, B gets FFTL and A gets FFTW; a scope that loads both must qualify the name.

GPU: large prime factors in multi-dimensional transforms ​

Decision (2026-11-14). In the KernelAbstractions extension:

  • Ragged lanes. A one-pass kernel takes the lines of a dimension in memory order with any number of lines per workgroup; the lanes past the last line repeat it and store nothing new. It is used where the old rule (the lines per workgroup divide the line count) leaves fewer than four lines per workgroup, and in the fused convolution kernels.

  • Rader's algorithm for a prime length whose   has prime factors up to 13 and fits one pass: the cyclic convolution of length   in one kernel, with the same structure as the fused Bluestein kernel (permuted loads and stores from a table, from the forward result). A single line of a prime above 512 keeps Bluestein's algorithm.

  • Balanced radices in the fused Bluestein and Rader kernels: the fewest stages of radices up to 16, the smallest largest radix among them (1008 as , 100 as ). Ordinary passes keep the chain of small radices.

  • Odd real transforms by a DFT of per two lines. r2c, c2r, DCT-II and DST-II of odd length pack two real lines into one complex line (Makhoul's permutation for DCT-II/DST-II) instead of a complex DFT of (DCT-II/DST-II) or of each real line (r2c/c2r). With a fused line plan the packing and the separation of the pair run inside that kernel.

  • On Apple GPUs the Bluestein length is the next power of two when it fits one pass.

Why. On a GPU, multi-dimensional transforms with a dimension of 101 or 1009 were 5–20× slower than cuFFT or VkFFT (harness dev runs on an NVIDIA RTX A6000 and an Apple M1 Max). The causes, measured one by one on the M1 Max:

  • A prime line count (101, 101²) left one line per workgroup, 14 threads for a pass of 224: passes of 128 and 224 along a stride of 101 ran at 145 GB/s, along a stride of 128 at 470 GB/s. With ragged lanes the stride of 101 runs at 470 GB/s.

  • Bluestein's convolution needs a length of at least   (224 or 256 for 101), Rader's   (100): 101³ dimension 2, 89 µs against 141 µs.

  • In the fused kernels the split of the length into radices decides the speed: one thread per butterfly of the largest radix, so a small radix next to 16 takes many rounds per thread (1009² dimension 1: 1008 as 141 µs, as 249 µs; 101³: 224 as 335 µs, 256 as 142 µs). A single line runs in one workgroup, where more threads win, so ordinary passes keep many small stages (r2c 3000, a line of 1500: 300 threads 41 µs, 100 threads 58 µs).

  • Code size: loops over long lines unrolled at generation (1009 elements, 84 threads) made the fused kernel slower than separate kernels; as loops in the kernel the packed kernel is faster.

Provenance. Rader's algorithm (1968) inside the radix kernels instead of Bluestein, and the limit of 13 for the prime factors of  , follow the VkFFT paper (D. Tolmachev, IEEE Access 11, 2023, §IV.A); the limit was confirmed by measurement (139, with    , is 1.7× slower with Rader). No VkFFT code was read. Ragged lanes, the packed odd real transforms and the radix split were found by measurement before the paper was read.

Not done. Rader for primes whose   has a factor above 13 (REDFT10 139×117×156 is at 0.92 of the fastest library on the M1 and 2.3× slower than it on the A6000), and for a   beyond one pass. The radix split of ordinary passes on many lines. The length rule for Bluestein on CUDA (224 kept there).

Scope ​

Decision (2026-11-01). Real transforms (r2c, c2r, all real-to-real kinds including halfcomplex), a KernelAbstractions GPU path (complex; Metal and CUDA tuned), a trimmed C library (libfftl, all CPU transforms) and, from 2026-11-02, generic float types are part of FFTL.

Why. Real passes are needed by NFFT.jl, and the GPU path reuses the generator and the plan structure. The parts that do not depend on the backend (codelet generation, factorisation, twiddles, pass structure) stay separate from the CPU execution so that the GPU path can share them.
