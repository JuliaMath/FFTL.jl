Design decisions ​

The decisions that shape FFTL, each with the reason and the evidence it rests on. A decision stays here until something replaces it; a replaced decision is marked, so a reader who finds the old reasoning elsewhere can see that it no longer holds. Numbers are measurements on an Apple M1 Max and on AMD EPYC (Zen 2) unless a line says otherwise.

Licence and provenance ​

Decision. FFTL is MIT-licensed. No GPL code is read, copied, translated or closely paraphrased. FFTW is a benchmark competitor only. Concepts known from the literature are used; code structure, naming and style deliberately do not follow FFTW (a 25-year-old C library with generated codelets and a measuring planner). Permissive sources may be read; a close take-over keeps its notice. The rules for contributors are on the Contribute page and in AGENTS.md.

Codelets with complex values in SIMD registers ​

Decision. A codelet works on complex values interleaved in SIMD registers. On aarch64 with the ARMv8.3 complex-number extension it uses FCADD and FCMLA, elsewhere shuffles (x86: vfmaddsub for products, addsub for ±i). Whether the extension is present is decided when the package is compiled: Apple silicon always, Linux by the fcma flag in /proc/cpuinfo (LLVM's host feature query returns nothing on macOS).

Why. On the M1 this layout with FCADD/FCMLA was the first variant that beat FFTW; keeping split real and imaginary parts in registers spilled for Float64. The layout is a per-platform choice behind dispatch on the vector type, so the generator and the plan structure stay shared.

A heuristic radix choice, no measuring planner ​

Decision. The radix sequence of a length comes from a fixed rule on its prime factorisation (src/factor.jl). There is no measuring planner by default.

Why. Brute-force searches over all decompositions put the best and the second-best within 1–3 %, and the large losses of an estimate-only planner are cache effects the algorithm has to avoid anyway. A per-machine calibration or an optional measuring mode can follow if the rule leaves too much on the table.

One such cache effect is part of the rule: single lines on AVX-512 take radix 16 only while the 16 streams of a stage are less than an L1 set stride (4 KB) apart, that is up to 4096 for Float32 and 2048 for Float64. Beyond that the streams share one cache set and radix 8 wins. AMD EPYC 9354, one line, all buffers at fixed page offsets, time of the radix-16 sequence over the radix-8 sequence: Float32 4096: 0.88, 8192: 1.06, 16384: 1.06; Float64 4096: 1.33, 8192: 1.03, 16384: 1.11.

Rules for lengths are derived on some lengths and checked on others ​

Decision (2026-11-04). The radix choice and the grid length of a resampled transform are rules on the prime factorisation, not tables per length. A rule is derived from one part of the lengths (a brute-force search over all decompositions, or a cost model) and checked on lengths that were not used for the derivation, for example by parity of n or by a random split; only the checked result counts as evidence. A mechanism with a stated cause (a cost estimate) is preferred over a list of lengths. Rules are per architecture where the search differs. After every change of a rule, the dense sweep is repeated and its distribution (median, share above 1.2×, 1.5×, 2×, worst lengths) is the criterion, not single points.

Why. The first rules were fitted to a few dozen lengths, mostly powers of two and smooth lengths on the M1. A dense sweep of every length 2…1024 against FFTW MEASURE (one thread, Zen 2, 2046 cases) showed 100 Float32 and 64 Float64 lengths slower than 1.5×, the worst at 3.4×.

Lengths with large prime factors: split off the smooth part, resample the rest ​

Decision (2026-11-07). For n = s·q, with q the part made of primes above MAX_RADIX_PRIME (73, at the time of the decision 31): one Cooley–Tukey step, a chain of length s, twiddles, and s resampled transforms of length q (Composite): one batched pass from 32 lanes on, lane by lane below (2026-11-12). A leftover q stays one resampled transform while its grid is at most 4 MiB; above that, q is split into its prime powers, recursively. Grids (and Bluestein convolutions) on one thread are transformed by a four-step where grid_fourstep says so: with 64-byte vectors where a line of their length would (see "Long lines: the four-step's rows go through cache buffers"), on other x86 machines from 8 MiB (1.3× from 2²⁰ on, Zen 2), not on aarch64, where the chain is faster up to 2²² (M1 Max). With threads, CompositeMT is used when s is at least the number of threads and is bitwise equal to the serial kernel; otherwise the whole length is one ResampleMT.

Why. The grids of length about 2q stay in cache. On 45 non-smooth lengths (Zen 2) FFTL moved from ×0.64 to ×1.04 of the fastest other library with one thread, and from ×0.77 to ×1.16 with 16 threads (measured with Bluestein's algorithm on the leftover). Resampling needs one transform of a length of at least 2q where Bluestein's algorithm needs two: it took 0.51–0.82 of Bluestein's time for primes from 1009 to 100003 (Tiger Lake, one thread), at a larger error in Float64 (see "Accuracy" below). The batched pass vectorises across the lanes and loses with few of them: against the lanes one by one it took 1.7–3.8 times as long at s = 2, 1.1–1.6 at s = 7, 0.73–1.04 at s = 16 and 0.56–1.04 at s = 32 (Float64, q = 37, 1009, 10007, EPYC 9354, one thread; Float32 2.1–7.3, 1.6–2.9, 0.84–1.31, 0.87–0.96). The switch is on the lane count and not on the number of threads, which keeps serial and threaded plans bitwise equal.

The grid of a single resampled transform has a margin on each side that holds copies of the other end (2026-11-12), so every output is interpolated from one contiguous load. Without it the outputs whose window wraps around the grid ends (7 per Float64 transform, 3 per Float32) took a scalar loop at about ten times the cost of the others: 250 of 559 ns at n = 37 and 290 of 1104 ns at n = 101. With the margin, time over the time without it: n = 37: 0.55, 101: 0.75, 127: 0.79, 1009: 0.96, 100003: 0.98, 101³: 0.78; against main: 0.81, 0.81, 0.77, 0.64, 0.80, 0.83, and 0.85 for 101³ on 8 threads (1.17 before). Float64, EPYC 9354, minima of interleaved runs, the same code against itself within 6 %. The margin is a whole page: a margin of 128 bytes moves the grid off the page offset of the work buffers, and the prime length 100003 then took 1.03–1.05 of the time without margins.

Against Bluestein's algorithm with the looped prime kernel in both (2026-11-14; main at 540d876, AMD EPYC 9354, one NUMA node of a shared machine, one process per variant and thread count, 3 rounds in rotating order, a second copy of main as A/A): over 73 one-thread cases (1-D lines up to 10⁷, planes, 3-D, rfft, run-time plans, composite dimensions) the geometric mean of the time ratio is 0.74, 55 cases faster beyond the A/A band and none slower; over 30–31 cases at 2, 4, 8, 16 and 32 threads 0.83, 0.66, 0.68, 0.68 and 0.66, none slower beyond the band (A/A median deviation 5–23 %, largest at 2, 4 and 32 threads). The seven cases with a median above 1.05 were measured again in 7 rounds: 0.60–1.01. 1009·2¹⁶ (Float64, 16 threads) took 165 ms instead of 552–555 ms: on main the second step, lane by lane, took 445 of 554 ms, almost all of it in writing each output 2¹⁶ values from the last (107 ms with the same outputs written contiguously); the batched pass writes runs of consecutive outputs. Error against FFTW in Float64 precision, largest over the cases: Float64 3.3·10⁻¹⁵ (Bluestein 1.0·10⁻¹⁵), Float32 1.8·10⁻⁷ (Bluestein 3.1·10⁻⁷).

Accuracy (2026-11-17): accepted, Float64 up to about 25 ε. Error = largest |y − ref| over the largest |ref|, against a reference in double-double arithmetic, in units of ε of the precision; primes 101–1000003, composites s·q, 101², 1009², 101³, rfft, brfft and DCT-II on such lengths.

Float64Float32
resampling, AMD EPYC 77026.7–23 ε (2-D, 3-D 11–24 ε)0.5–1.9 ε

The cause is the deconvolution, not the window: the input is multiplied by 1/ψ̂, which spans a factor of about 8 over the band at w = 16 and m ≈ 2n, and the rounding of the grid transform is amplified by that factor. The truncation error of the window is below ε there. A wider window raises β and the factor (14 at w = 20, 25 at w = 24): w = 20 gave 19 ε rms and w = 24 39–54 ε rms at m ≈ 2n (w = 16: 16 ε rms), whatever β. A larger grid lowers the factor (3.4 at m ≈ 2.5n, 2.3 at 3n): m ≈ 2.5n gave 8.5–14 ε in 1-D and 18 ε for 1009² (the errors of the dimensions add up) at 1.12–1.27 times the time, m ≈ 3n no better at 1.16–1.42. On x86 the grid length is the fastest smooth length in [2n, 2.8n], so the factor and the error vary with n (101: grid 256, 8.7 ε). The rms error does not grow with q (15.4–15.8 ε rms for 1009 to 1000003); the maximum grows slowly with the number of outputs. Bluestein's algorithm for Float64 on x86 as well would take 1.07–1.55 times the time of resampling on most cases (EPYC 7702, one thread, 3 interleaved rounds). The bound is stated in the docstring of Resample, and the tests check 40 ε (Float64) and 4 ε (Float32) against a 128-bit reference.

Preference (2026-11-17). The default stays resampling; float64_large_primes = "bluestein" (FFTL.FLOAT64_LARGE_PRIMES, read at precompile time like simd_bytes) makes every Float64 kernel of such a length Bluestein's: single lines (bluestein_lines), composite leftovers of any lane count (no RsBatch) and the threaded long line (BluesteinMT, the kernel of FFTL 0.2, in place of ResampleMT). Float32 resamples in both modes. CI runs the tests in both modes, with the bound 8 ε instead of 40 ε for Float64; measured 2.1–4.3 ε in this mode on the M1 Max (batched leftover 32·1009, threaded 20011 and 100003, 101³ included) against 20–24 ε by default. Time with the preference over the default on the M1 Max (Float64, 3 interleaved rounds, a loaded machine): batched leftovers 32·79, 32·1009, 64·1009 on one thread 1.20–1.31, but 64·10007 0.72 (the batched pass is slower than Bluestein's lanes there; not followed up); long lines on eight threads 100003, 1000003, 1009·2¹², rfft 2·100003 1.24–1.57. On x86 see the times above (1.07–1.55 on most cases).

On the same machine Bluestein's algorithm (v0.2.0) was faster than resampling for 10007 and 16·10007 (0.87–0.89 of the time, c2c, rfft, brfft, DCT-II); not followed up.

Replaced. Bluestein's algorithm on the leftover and BluesteinMT for a whole line on threads (2026-11-07). Generic number types and the GPU passes keep Bluestein's algorithm.

Exception (2026-11-16): Float64 on aarch64 keeps Bluestein's algorithm for single lines (bluestein_lines): a whole line of a non-smooth length, a composite leftover lane by lane (fewer than 32 lanes), and the run-time plans. With two Float64 values per NEON register the 16-tap interpolation costs more than Bluestein's second transform. Apple M1 Max, one thread, resampled against Bluestein (main at 540d876): Float64 1.18–1.53 for 10007, rfft 20014, 100003, 101² and 127², Float32 0.60–0.84. The batched leftover pass (RsBatch, from 32 lanes) and the threaded kernels (ResampleMT, CompositeMT with the batched pass) resample in both precisions; they were faster in Float64 as well (64·1009 one thread 0.80; 1000003 on 8 threads 0.72). With the exception, against main at 1ae621e (M1 Max, minimum over 5 blocks, 2 rounds in alternating processes, a loaded machine): Float64 one thread 0.96–1.02 for the single lines above, 0.79 for 64·1009 and 0.85 for 64·79; Float32 one thread 0.57–0.81; smooth lengths 0.97–1.01. On x86 nothing changes.

Not done / not checked. Rader's algorithm (5 of the 45 sweep lengths qualify). Both thresholds were measured on Zen 2 only, and the 4 MiB rule is checked on 12 lengths that also took part in its derivation. The 32-lane rule was measured on one AVX-512 machine only, not on AVX2 or aarch64, and the grid margin on x86 only (512-bit and 256-bit vectors), not on aarch64.

Plan tables of long lines from a few exact values ​

Decision (2026-11-14). Two tables that a plan builds over the indices of a long line no longer take one transcendental call per entry:

  • The deconvolution factors of a resampled transform (resample_corr) are a sum of 26 (Float64) or 18 (Float32) cosines over the quadrature nodes, each sampled at consecutive indices t. With t = t0 + k (t0 a multiple of 64), every cosine is one product of two correctly rounded values: one sincospi per node and block of 64, and a table over k shared by all blocks. The node weights a_q = w_q ψ(z_q) are computed once, not per index, and the factors are even in t, so half of them are computed.

  • The factor tables of a long four-step (above TUNING[].factor_twiddles) take their n1 + n2 roots from unit_root, which applies the symmetries of unit_roots to one index. The tables are bitwise the same as before, without the table of all n roots they were taken from (2 GiB for 2²⁷).

Why. Planning a prime length near 2²⁶ took 32 s, 30 of them in the deconvolution factors (67·10⁶ indices, 26 exp, sqrt and cospi each). AMD EPYC 9354, 16 threads, a fresh plan with the cache cleared, 2 rounds in alternating processes, before → after:

2²⁶FourStep0.81 s → 0.002 s

resample_corr alone for 2²⁶ − 5 on one thread: 30.2 s → 0.68 s (Float32 0.43 s). Against 256-bit arithmetic (the same quadrature rule; n from 2 to 2²⁶ − 5, both ends of the index range and random indices), the product form has the error of one cospi per term: median 2.4–2.7 ulp, at most 8.9 ulp in Float64 for both forms; in Float32 the factors are bitwise the same for n up to 10⁶. A rotation recurrence (one complex multiply per step) is as fast but its error grows along the block: 1.2·10⁻¹³ after 512 steps. Transform times are unchanged (the four-step's output is bitwise the same).

Not done. The twiddles of the composite kernel (Composite.tw, CompositeMT.tw) are still a table of all n roots: 1 GiB and 1.4 s of planning for 1009·2¹⁶. The kernel reads it at run time, so factor tables there change the kernel.

One chain form: the radix sequence is data ​

Decision (2026-11-13). Every smooth length runs on RChain: the radix sequence and the stage records are data, the codelets come from a fixed set. There is no chain type with the radix sequence in its type, and nothing is compiled per length. RChain is also the only form the trimmed C library can contain.

Why. The compiled chain (Chain{T,Rs,σ,K}, used for smooth lengths up to 128 until this decision) existed because the run-time chain cost 9–20 ns more per call on x86. That cost was not the choice of the codelet at run time (about 1 ns per stage) but the code around it: GC frames for references loaded from the chain, the copy of an immutable chain into each caller, bounds checks, and one more call level per stage. With those removed (see Implementation) the compiled chain has no advantage left. AMD EPYC 9354 (AVX-512), one thread, plan_fft and mul!, 92 cases (Float64 and Float32; lines of 2 to 4096, planes of 8 × 8 to 128 × 128, cubes of 8³ to 64³, in-place, backward and real transforms), 8 rounds in alternating processes, median time over the median time with the compiled chain:

  • geometric mean 0.973; 27 cases below 0.95, 8 above 1.05;

  • lines of one stage (2, 4, 16): 0.80–0.95; 8 × 8, 8 × 8 × 8, 7 × 12, 5 × 64: 0.85–0.91;

  • the cases above 1.05 are short lines of two or three stages: Float32 20, 32, 96 and 100 (1.09–1.10), 37 rows of 64 (1.13), rfft of 64 (1.07 and 1.09), and Float64 12 × 7 (1.05). The stage loop runs about 50 instructions per stage around the codelet (no mispredicted branches), 2.4 ns per stage against direct calls of the two kernels of Float32 32.

  • The machine was shared during the measurement: over the 8 rounds the slowest time of the compiled chain over its fastest is 1.04 in the median over the cases, 1.19 at the 90th percentile and 1.55 at most.

Not verified on aarch64.

What the single form removes: 583 compiled chain methods and their generators, the dispatch trees from an RChain of a short length into the compiled chains, and the compilation at the first plan of a short length with a radix sequence outside the compiled set (a short dimension with a large stride). The package has 1796 methods instead of 2385, the package image on this machine is 61 MB instead of 72 MB, and libfftl.so (x86-64-v3, --trim=safe) is 49.5 MB instead of 55.6 MB.

Replaced. The two chain forms (2026-11-09: compiled Chain for smooth lengths up to 128, RChain for the rest), and before that the idea of removing Chain without changing the stage loop (2026-11-08) and the separate JIT and run-time plan families (2026-11-02).

Kernels of a fixed set are plain methods, not @generated ​

Decision (2026-11-10, fallback removed 2026-11-13). The stage kernels of the codelet set that RChain dispatches to are plain methods, defined with @eval in src/kernel_methods.jl from the functions that build their bodies. One table (STAGE_KERNELS in src/kernels.jl) lists the kernels and their arguments; the methods and the dispatch switches of RChain are made from it. There are no @generated stage kernels: every combination the chain can select has a plain method, so a fallback would never run.

Why. A package image keeps the complete optimised IR of every precompiled @generated specialisation, 130 MB of the 146 MB image. See Package size and load time.

The first stage of a single line runs at full vector width ​

Decision (2026-11-12). Where the widest vector holds 8 complex values (Float32 on AVX-512), the first stage of a single line runs stage_small!: vectorised over the butterflies, with the outputs interleaved in registers into contiguous stores. It exists for the power-of-two radices and belongs to the fixed set of plain methods.

Why. The grouped first stage handles at most 4 complex values per vector. Intel Core i7-1185G7, one line, Float32, lengths 128–16384 with radices of 16s and 8s: 12–26 % faster (the same code against itself differs by up to 3 %).

Not done. The same for later stages with a stride of 2 or 4. It needs a per-lane twiddle table and one kernel per stride, and only Float32 lengths whose radices start with 4, 4 have such a stage. On an AMD EPYC 9354 it gained 6–8 % at 192 and 320 and nothing measurable at other lengths.

Stage kernels keep twiddles in memory and address streams from few registers ​

Decision (2026-11-12). In the stages vectorised over q, the twiddle multiply reads re(w) and im(w) from the table (ccmul_at; memory operands on x86), and the R input and R output streams are addressed as base + odd·stride·2^e from one base per 8 streams (stream_addr). The base pointer is hidden from LLVM's loop optimiser (opaque_ptr), which otherwise goes back to one pointer per stream. The base is formed in the integer domain (inttoptr of the address), which scalar evolution does not analyse; the same stage kernels as with llvm.launder.invariant.group (inner loops identical up to register names and, in three Float32 range kernels, instruction order; AMD EPYC 9354, Julia 1.13). The scaled addresses stay a typed getelementptr in llvmcall: Julia's pointer arithmetic is a byte GEP of a separate shift, which LLVM hoists and merges into an invalid x86 scale, and the middle stages of radix 8 and 16 then spill (radix 16, Float64: 196 → 224–232 instructions per inner loop). On Julia 1.10 (LLVM 15, typed pointers, a Ptr passed to llvmcall as an integer) scaled_ptr is the same GEP written with typed pointers between inttoptr and ptrtoint; LLVM folds the conversions into the address of the load. The kernels compiled by 1.10 are not the same as those of 1.13 (some are unrolled, and some range and middle stages of radix 5, 7 and 16 reload values from the stack); 1.10 is supported for correctness, its speed is not tuned.

Why. A radix-16 stage has 15 twiddles and 32 streams, more than fit in registers, so its inner loop reloaded vectors and pointers from the stack. AMD EPYC 9354 (AVX-512), instructions per butterfly: radix 16 middle stage 284 → 198, radix 8 100 → 79. One line, plan_fft, medians of 8 interleaved rounds: lengths 512–16384 take 0.79–0.94 (Float32) and 0.82–0.92 (Float64) of the time, lengths from 32768 on are unchanged (the same code against itself differs by 1–5 %). Not verified on aarch64.

Not done. Radix 16 as two radix-4 passes (the first into the output slots, the second in place on them, blocked so that a block stays in L1), which needs no addressing tricks: its inner loops do not spill, but the extra pass through L1 costs what radix 16 saves over radix 8. AMD EPYC 9354, one line, medians of 5–6 interleaved rounds, against the one-pass radix 16: Float32 256, 2048, 4096: 1.24, 1.24, 1.31; Float64 256, 2048: 1.17, 1.05 (same code against itself: 0.99–1.02), and slower than the best radix-4/8 chain at each length. With 256-bit vectors and 16 registers (-C haswell, simd_bytes = 32) 1.05–1.08 against the one-pass radix 16, and both lose to radix-4/8 chains by 7–20 %.

The prime radices from 17 to 73 share one looped kernel ​

Decision (2026-11-13). A stage of a prime radix from 17 on runs prime_stage! (src/primestage.jl): one kernel for all such radices, with the radix and a table of cosines and sines as arguments, compiled once per vector type. It uses the symmetric form of the odd-prime codelet as loops and computes up to eight output pairs (four with 16 vector registers) in one pass over the inputs. It covers the stage kinds that the codelets have separate kernels for: stages vectorised over the lanes, the last stage, lane blocks, and the grouped first stage. MAX_RADIX_PRIME is 73. The radices up to 16 keep their straight-line codelets. The KernelAbstractions kernels and the generic number types keep their limit of 31.

Why. A straight-line codelet of a prime r has about r²/2 statements with as many different constants, which do not fit in registers (525 statements for 31), and every radix needed 82 kernel methods on AVX-512 (kind × sign × vector type). The codelets of 17–31 were therefore compiled at first use and not with the package (decision of 2026-11-10, replaced by this one). With the loop, a prime costs a table.

Measurements: AMD EPYC 9354 (AVX-512), one thread, plan_fft and mul!, rounds in alternating processes, median time over the median time of the previous code.

  • Lengths and shapes with a factor 17–31 against the codelets (40 cases, 4 rounds): geometric mean 0.82, 36 cases below 0.95, one above 1.05 (Float64 100 × 31, 1.06: a single stage in place, which copies the inputs of a butterfly first). 17 and 31 alone: 0.63–0.84; 1088 = 17·64: 0.96 and 0.89; 837 = 27·31 and 841 = 29²: 0.72–0.82; 62 × 62: 0.70–0.74.

  • Long lengths (36 cases, 3 rounds; 31·2⁶ to 17·2²⁰, and a factor 17–31 times a prime above 1000): geometric mean 0.94, one case above 1.05 (Float64 19·2¹⁷, 1.08). 31·2⁶ to 31·2¹⁴: 0.74–0.91; 17·2²⁰: 0.99 and 1.01.

  • The primes 37–73 against Bluestein's algorithm, which they used before (100 cases, 2 rounds; p, 8p, 64p, 1024p, 16384p): geometric mean 0.29. The prime alone: 0.43–0.79 (Float64) and 0.48–0.90 (Float32); as a factor of a longer length: 0.13–0.53.

  • 92 general cases without such factors (the set of the decision above, 4 rounds): geometric mean 1.00 without the two cases with a dimension of 37 (0.10 and 0.16), 3 cases above 1.05 (Float64 lines of 32, 48 and 60: 1.07–1.15). In 6 rounds on 20 short lines alone these three were at 1.04, 0.83 and 1.05 and the geometric mean 1.00; the stage kernels of these lengths compile to the same machine code as before.

  • Accuracy against a 200-bit reference, Float64, 841, 6256 and 6859: 1.4–1.8 ε, with the codelets 3.5–4.6 ε (the table is built from angles reduced to one turn).

What made the loop as fast as the unrolled sums: several rows per pass, so that the inputs are loaded once for all of them and the multiply-add chains of the rows run side by side; no buffer for the sums and differences of the inputs (storing whole registers was the slowest part: 1.3–1.4× per butterfly with the buffer); the pass over a block of rows out of line and the outputs of a block written by recursion over the tuples of accumulators (a loop with an index into them, or a closure over them, put the accumulators on the stack: up to 1.4× for a whole transform).

The limit. With the limit at 127 for the measurement, a prime from 79 on is still 1.3–4× faster than Bluestein's algorithm as a factor of a longer length, but the line of that prime alone is slower from 79 on for Float32 (1.05, 1.5 at 127) and from 97 on for Float64 (1.01, 1.15 at 127). 73 is the largest prime for which every measured case is faster.

Against resampling, which replaced Bluestein's algorithm (2026-11-14, EPYC 9354, one thread, the kernels called directly, median of 3 rounds): as a factor of 64p the looped kernel takes 0.29–0.44 of the composite kernel's time for every prime p from 37 to 73, in both precisions. A line of the prime alone in Float64 takes 0.59–0.97 of the resampled time (0.97 at 73). In Float32 resampling is faster from 47 on: the looped kernel takes 1.10–1.96 times as long (1.96 at 73), at 37–43 they are even (0.91–1.08). The looped kernel takes the same time in both precisions, the resampled transform 0.49–0.64 of its Float64 time in Float32. The limit stays at 73; a rule for Float32 lines of a prime alone is not done.

Not done. The loop for 11 and 13: with one kernel per radix (an earlier form of the loop) it was at 0.98 of the codelets for Float64 and 1.05–1.06 for Float32, and 1.08–1.10 for 7; not measured with the single kernel. A larger limit for lengths that are not a prime alone. aarch64 is not verified.

Costs. The kernel and its tables are in the package image, which the lazy codelets were not: the image file is 66 MB instead of 59 MB on this machine (68.5 MB before the decision on the chain form). The C library contains every kernel in either case and shrinks: libfftl.so (x86-64-v3, --trim=safe) is 26.8 MB instead of 49.5 MB, its code 8.8 MB instead of 18.6 MB.

Long lines: the four-step's rows go through cache buffers ​

Decision (2026-11-14). The four-step (FourStep, src/threads.jl) works on blocks of columns that pass through a buffer in cache:

  • With 64-byte vectors, step 1 copies a block's rows into the buffer and transforms them there; no butterfly stage reads the input with its row stride n1 (FOURSTEP_COPY).

  • The twiddle multiplication and the transposition into the intermediate array A are done in registers, C × C complex values at a time (ctranspose, C values per vector), and A is written with non-temporal stores where each store is a whole aligned line (64-byte vectors; the rows of A are padded by 64 bytes).

  • With 64-byte vectors, step 2 writes its output through the buffer with non-temporal stores from TUNING[].fourstep_nt_bytes (8 MiB) of output; below that, and with narrower vectors, its last stage writes the output.

  • A block's rows are at least 128 bytes on several threads (TUNING[].fourstep_row_bytes; Float32 takes 16 columns, Float64 8) and 256 bytes on one thread.

  • With 64-byte vectors, a single line on one thread takes the four-step from 16 times the last-level cache of a core, in Float32 for powers of two only (serial_fourstep); the grids of resampling and Bluestein's algorithm follow the same rule (grid_fourstep; elsewhere from 8 MiB on x86 and never on aarch64).

Why. AMD EPYC 9354 (32 MiB L3 per four cores), measurements in one process with the variants alternating unless noted:

  • A large power of two was the slow case: 2²⁶ Float64 took 1.6–1.8 times as long per element as 10⁸ (16 threads and one thread), in both steps, while its chains in cache cost about the same. The chains' first stage read the input with a stride of 2¹³ values. Without the copy, 2²⁶ took 1.41 times as long (16 threads).

  • Writing A cost about as much as the chains (35–48 % of step 1 on one thread), one complex value per instruction. In registers: 1.7–2.2 times faster (Float64), 3–3.6 (Float32) alone. In the whole transform with ordinary stores it was slower for Float32 at 2²² and 3·2²⁰ (16 threads, step 1 0.36 → 0.50 ns per element): step 2 reads A on other threads, so the lines of A are still held by other cores when step 1 writes them, and every store first takes ownership. With the lines read by other threads first, the vectorised writes took 7 times as long as on untouched lines. Non-temporal whole-line stores need no ownership: time with them over the time without, 16 threads, Float32 2¹³ 0.90, 2¹⁶ 0.83, 2²² 0.67, 6¹⁰ 0.89; Float64 2¹⁶ 0.81, 10⁶ 0.54, 2²² 0.73, 2²⁶ 0.96; 4 threads 0.56–0.85.

  • Step 2 without non-temporal output: 1.05–1.32 times as long in Float64 from 16 MB, 1.01–1.14 in Float32 from 8 MiB; with a threshold of 4 MiB instead of 8, Float64 2¹⁸ took 0.87 of the time (not adopted: the output then leaves the cache, which a benchmark of the transform alone does not charge). Writing the output through the buffer with ordinary stores was never better than the last stage writing it.

  • Rows of 128 bytes for Float32 on 16 threads: 0.86–0.88 at 2²⁴ and 6¹⁰, 0.99–1.04 elsewhere. 256-byte rows: 1.06–1.19 times as long for Float64 at 2²⁶ and 6¹⁰. On one thread 256-byte rows took 2²⁶ Float64 from 14.1 to 11.5 ns per element.

  • Copying the rows below 16 MiB costs 1–5 % (Float32 10⁶ 0.95 without it, Float64 2¹⁶ and 2¹⁸ 0.96), within the noise of transforms of a millisecond; the copy is unconditional.

  • One thread, four-step time over chain time: Float64 2¹⁹ 1.88, 2²² 1.08, 2²⁴ 0.96, 3·2²³ 0.81, 2²⁵ 0.80, 2²⁶ 0.64, 6¹⁰ 0.98, 10⁸ 0.95, three lengths near 2²⁵ with power-of-two parts 2¹¹–2¹⁷ 0.88–0.97; Float32 2²⁰ 1.61, 2²⁵ 1.09, 2²⁶ 0.94, 2²⁷ 0.76, 10⁸ 1.17, three lengths near 2²⁶ with power-of-two parts 2¹²–2¹⁸ 1.11–1.16. Beyond the cache a chain makes one pass over memory per stage (10–13 at these lengths) at 34 GB/s on one core, 22 GB/s for 2²⁶ (strides); the four-step's work in cache costs about as much as 13 such passes. From 512 MiB the rule takes no measured case where the four-step is slower; Float64 lengths of 384–506 MiB (0.81–0.97) stay on the chain.

  • Against main (2026-11-14, plan_fft and mul!, one process per variant and thread count with as many threads in Julia's pool, 2 rounds in rotating order, A/A within 2.9 % at 4 threads and 0.8 % at 16): 22 cases each (2¹⁶–2²⁶, 10⁶, 6¹⁰, 3·2²⁰, the prime 1000003, rfft 2²², both precisions), none slower; time ratio 0.46–0.81 at 4 threads (2²⁶ 0.55–0.57), 0.58–0.85 at 16 (2²⁶ 0.58–0.59); geometric mean of the ratio normalised by FFTW in the same process 0.62 and 0.69.

Not done / not checked. The radix sequence inside the four-step: radix 8 or 16 for the chains of a block (the rule given the block's stride, or the single-line rule) was not consistently faster, mostly 1.00–1.24 times the current time; chains of 2048 with radix 8 took 0.89–0.90 (8 threads). The lane-block kernels themselves are slower than single lines of the same length (1.1–1.9 times per element in cache for powers of two), which is the larger gain left. A three-factor (nested) four-step for lengths whose blocks outgrow L2. The multiple of the cache for the one-thread rule and every threshold here are measured on this machine only; 32 threads could not be measured on the shared machine (one thread of the pool waits for a core).

Limited to 64-byte vectors (2026-11-16). The one-thread rule, the copy in step 1 and the non-temporal output hold only with 64-byte vectors, where they were measured. On an Apple M1 Max (12 MiB last-level cache) the one-thread rule took 2²⁴ Float64 to the four-step at 1.59 times the chain's time, and the copy cost 5–15 % (4 threads, in one process, without it: Float64 0.85–0.92, Float32 0.90–1.01 at 2¹⁶–2²⁴, 10⁶ and 6⁸). The register transpose and the row widths hold everywhere: on the M1 the transpose took Float32 to 0.86–0.92 of the loop over single values (Float64, one value per vector, 0.99–1.00), and row widths of 16–128 bytes were within 3 %. Against main before the four-step changes (M1 Max, 2 rounds in alternating processes, a second copy of main as A/A): one thread 0.94–1.04, 4 threads Float64 within the A/A spread and Float32 0.87–0.92 at 2²², 3·2²⁰, 2²⁴ and 10⁶. AMD EPYC 7763 (AVX2, 32-byte vectors: only the register transpose and the row widths apply), one socket pinned, same protocol: one thread 0.96–1.03 (A/A 0.94–1.08); 4 and 16 threads Float64 0.86–1.03, Float32 0.70–0.87 (A/A 0.85–1.10).

Run-time pass plans for trimmed programs ​

Decision (2026-11-10). A pass plan (plan_pass, plan_r2c_pass, plan_c2r_pass) built with _runtime (plan_pass_runtime, plan_r2c_pass_runtime, plan_c2r_pass_runtime) uses run-time kernels only: RChain, resampled and composite transforms over RChain, the four-step algorithm over RChain. Its type depends on the element type and the number of dimensions, not on the length. The specialised plans are unchanged.

Why. A library that holds passes of many lengths in one container (as NFFT.jl does for its boxes) has a container of an abstract or length-dependent type, and juliac --trim cannot resolve the calls through it. The first attempt was a keyword runtime = true; the verifier then still checks the specialised branch, whose kernel types depend on the length, so the separate functions were needed. A probe library compiled with juliac --trim=safe holds run-time pass plans in a vector, applies them, and matches the direct DFT to 3·10⁻¹⁴ when called from C. Planning on 64 × 64 × 64 views is 1.8 µs for d = 1 and 0.9 µs for d = 2 on the M1 (the specialised plan: 4.7 µs and 2.8 µs). Applying the specialised and the run-time plans is within noise of each other on the same views.

Costs. For real lengths with large prime factors, the run-time kernel is one resampled transform, without the composite split of the specialised plan.

Kernel unions are split by hand (0.3.1). The kernel field of a run-time plan is the union RPassKern, ten concrete types since 0.3.0 (the leftover of a composite is batched or lane by lane, with table or polynomial weights, or Bluestein). Inference splits unions of at most four types; above that the closures that run the lines on the tasks and the Composite constructor became dynamic calls, and juliac --trim=safe rejected them. runtime_kernel and run_pass! therefore branch on each concrete type with isa, so every call below the branch is static. libfftl does not build run-time pass plans and did not show the regression; libfftl/probe/ (a vector of run-time pass plans of Float32/Float64, complex and real, applied with mul! and called from C) is built in the libfftl workflow with --trim=safe and keeps these paths trimmable. A new kernel type in RPassKern needs a branch in both functions.

x86 instruction sets come from the code generation target ​

Decision (2026-11-20). HAS_AVX2, HAS_AVX512 and HAS_ZMM (512-bit registers, 64-byte kernels) require the feature both in CPUID and in Julia's code generation target. The target is checked with Core.Intrinsics.have_fma and by compiling three small functions and searching the machine code: a 256-bit integer addition (vpaddd ymm, AVX2), a 512-bit addition (a zmm register) and an unsigned integer to Float32 conversion (vcvtudq2ps, AVX-512F). The 512-bit addsub is the generic fma(c, (-1, 1, …), a) instead of the vfmaddsub intrinsic: the same exact result in one instruction, and LLVM can split it.

Why. Julia can compile for fewer features than the CPU reports. Since LLVM 18, 512-bit registers need the feature evex512, which LLVM derives from the CPU name; Julia 1.13 compiles for znver3 on a Zen 4 Ryzen 5 PRO 215, so the target has the AVX-512 instructions but no 512-bit registers, and FFTL 0.3.1, which read CPUID only, aborted while precompiling (issue #59: "LLVM ERROR: Do not know how to split the result of this operator!"). -C gives the same kind of mismatch: with -C x86-64 on an AVX2 machine the SSE3 addsub intrinsic failed ("Cannot select: X86ISD::ADDSUB"). A list of CPU names without 512-bit registers (#61) fixed the reported machine; the compiled probes replace it because they need no update for new CPU names and also cover -C. Julia exports no query for the target's features besides FMA. llc 20 gives the expected probe results for x86-64, sandybridge, haswell, znver3, znver3 with the AVX-512 features (instructions but no zmm), znver4, skylake-avx512 and x86-64-v4. CI runs the tests with -C x86-64 and -C haswell; the second covers an AVX-512 target only when the runner's AVX-512 is usable, which the test log reports.

Costs. Three small compilations at precompile time. aarch64 is unchanged: FCMA still comes from the platform (Apple) or /proc/cpuinfo, so a target without it needs cpu_features = "baseline".

The interface: FFTW.jl's names, FFTL's own functions, AbstractFFTs as a backend ​

Decision (2026-11-06). FFTL exports FFTW.jl's names (fft, bfft, ifft, rfft, brfft, irfft, the plan_ and in-place forms, the dct family, and the AbstractFFTs helpers), so using FFTL can take the place of using FFTW. r2r, the kinds, the flags and set_num_threads stay public with the prefix, as in FFTW.jl. The DFT and DCT functions are FFTL's own, not AbstractFFTs's; the package only does import AbstractFFTs, never using AbstractFFTs or import AbstractFFTs: fft. Generic code that calls AbstractFFTs.fft reaches FFTL through methods of plan_fft and its relatives for AbstractArrays of floating-point numbers (src/abstractffts.jl), which are less specific than FFTW.jl's StridedArray methods: with FFTW loaded, the generic functions take FFTW for its arrays and FFTL for the rest.

Why, and what was rejected.

  • Exported names that are AbstractFFTs's functions (re-exported bindings) made FFTL.fft === FFTW.fft: any dependency that loads FFTW would take the standard arrays away from code that wrote using FFTL, and the GPU extension's methods would have landed next to CUDA.jl's.

  • Methods with FFTW.jl's exact signatures: the package loaded last overwrites the other for the whole process, and a package depending on both fails to precompile ("Method overwriting is not permitted during Module precompilation").

  • Methods are global per process, so this is the only arrangement in which a scope with using FFTL reaches FFTL whatever a dependency loads. Tested: with B -> FFTL and B -> A -> FFTW, B gets FFTL and A gets FFTW; a scope that loads both must qualify the name.

GPU: large prime factors in multi-dimensional transforms ​

Decision (2026-11-14). In the KernelAbstractions extension:

  • Ragged lanes. A one-pass kernel takes the lines of a dimension in memory order with any number of lines per workgroup; the lanes past the last line repeat it and store nothing new. It is used where the old rule (the lines per workgroup divide the line count) leaves fewer than four lines per workgroup, and in the fused convolution kernels.

  • Rader's algorithm for a prime length whose   has prime factors up to 13 and fits one pass: the cyclic convolution of length   in one kernel, with the same structure as the fused Bluestein kernel (permuted loads and stores from a table, from the forward result). A single line of a prime above 512 keeps Bluestein's algorithm.

  • Balanced radices in the fused Bluestein and Rader kernels: the fewest stages of radices up to 16, the smallest largest radix among them (1008 as , 100 as ). Ordinary passes keep the chain of small radices.

  • Odd real transforms by a DFT of per two lines. r2c, c2r, DCT-II and DST-II of odd length pack two real lines into one complex line (Makhoul's permutation for DCT-II/DST-II) instead of a complex DFT of (DCT-II/DST-II) or of each real line (r2c/c2r). With a fused line plan the packing and the separation of the pair run inside that kernel.

  • On Apple GPUs the Bluestein length is the next power of two when it fits one pass.

Why. On a GPU, multi-dimensional transforms with a dimension of 101 or 1009 were 5–20× slower than cuFFT or VkFFT (harness dev runs on an NVIDIA RTX A6000 and an Apple M1 Max). The causes, measured one by one on the M1 Max:

  • A prime line count (101, 101²) left one line per workgroup, 14 threads for a pass of 224: passes of 128 and 224 along a stride of 101 ran at 145 GB/s, along a stride of 128 at 470 GB/s. With ragged lanes the stride of 101 runs at 470 GB/s.

  • Bluestein's convolution needs a length of at least   (224 or 256 for 101), Rader's   (100): 101³ dimension 2, 89 µs against 141 µs.

  • In the fused kernels the split of the length into radices decides the speed: one thread per butterfly of the largest radix, so a small radix next to 16 takes many rounds per thread (1009² dimension 1: 1008 as 141 µs, as 249 µs; 101³: 224 as 335 µs, 256 as 142 µs). A single line runs in one workgroup, where more threads win, so ordinary passes keep many small stages (r2c 3000, a line of 1500: 300 threads 41 µs, 100 threads 58 µs).

  • Code size: loops over long lines unrolled at generation (1009 elements, 84 threads) made the fused kernel slower than separate kernels; as loops in the kernel the packed kernel is faster.

GPU: small real transforms in one launch ​

Decision (2026-11-15). A real transform along the first dimension (r2c, c2r, DCT-II, DST-II) runs in a single kernel launch wherever its complex DFT is a single kernel:

  • Odd lengths: a plain one-pass kernel packs two real lines into one complex line, as the fused kernels already did.

  • Even lengths whose half is a Rader or one-pass Bluestein length: that fused kernel reads the real line as a complex line of half the length (Makhoul's permutation for DCT-II and DST-II) and separates the result in its last loop.

  • Less host work per call: no tuple slices and a function barrier in the r2r mul!, and user arrays reinterpreted on the device instead of on the host.

Why. Small GPU transforms are bound by the host's launch rate. On an NVIDIA RTX A6000 with an AMD EPYC 7763 host, a raw CUDA launch costs about 2.3 µs of host time and a KernelAbstractions launch about 3.8 µs, while the kernels of these transforms run for 1.5–3 µs. FFTL ran a pack kernel, the DFT and a post kernel, three launches where cuFFT and VkFFT use one: c2r 21 Float32 took 14 µs against 2.3 µs.

Scope ​

Decision (2026-11-01). Real transforms (r2c, c2r, all real-to-real kinds including halfcomplex), a KernelAbstractions GPU path (complex; Metal and CUDA tuned), a trimmed C library (libfftl, all CPU transforms) and, from 2026-11-02, generic float types are part of FFTL.

Why. Real passes are needed by NFFT.jl, and the GPU path reuses the generator and the plan structure. The parts that do not depend on the backend (codelet generation, factorisation, twiddles, pass structure) stay separate from the CPU execution so that the GPU path can share them.
