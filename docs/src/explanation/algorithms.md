# Algorithms

This page goes from the mathematics of [the DFT](@ref "The discrete Fourier transform") to what
FFTL runs. Each transform in the library is built from a few pieces, and the same pieces appear
in the complex, real and real-to-real paths.

## Stockham chains

A transform of length ``n = r_1 r_2 \cdots r_s`` is a sequence of ``s`` stages. Stage ``t``
combines ``r_t`` interleaved sub-transforms with twiddle factors and writes its result to a
second buffer, and the two buffers swap roles at each stage. This is the Stockham formulation,
which keeps the data in natural order throughout:

- no bit reversal or digit reversal at the start or the end,
- each stage reads and writes with regular strides,
- the radix sequence ``r_1, …, r_s`` is a single object that the plan carries.

The radices are chosen by a fixed rule (`src/factor.jl`). The rule is the result of exhaustive
searches over the decompositions of many lengths on the development machines, not of a runtime
search. It prefers radix 4 and radix 8 for powers of two. On aarch64 it merges a factor 3 with a 4
into a 12; the x86 rules differ and are in the same file. Every other prime up to 31 is a radix of
its own. Larger primes are not radices; the part of the length they make up goes to Bluestein's
algorithm (below).

## Codelets

A stage of radix ``r`` is a small DFT applied to ``r`` values at once. For each ``r`` the codelet
is straight-line code without loops, generated by a symbolic generator (`src/codegen.jl`). The
generator writes the DFT as a sum of terms and simplifies it:

- multiplications by ``\pm i`` are rotations of the term, not multiplications; two terms with
  rotations that differ by an odd power of ``i`` combine in one complex add-and-rotate;
- a composite radix ``r = r_1 r_2`` is split in registers with the usual index mapping;
- an odd prime uses the symmetric form: ``X_k, X_{r-k} = A ± i B`` with the cosine sums ``A``
  and the sine sums ``B``, which pairs the outputs ``k`` and ``r-k``.

The generated code runs on complex SIMD vectors (`src/complexvec.jl`), with the vector width of
the element type. On aarch64 the complex add-and-rotate and the complex multiply are single
instructions (`FCADD`, `FCMLA`).

## Bluestein's algorithm

Lengths with a prime factor above 31 go through Bluestein's identity. With ``jk = (j^2 + k^2 - (k-j)^2)/2``,

```math
X_k = c_k \sum_{j} (x_j c_j)\, \overline{c_{k-j}}, \qquad c_j = e^{\sigma \pi i j^2/n}.
```

The sum is a cyclic convolution with a kernel that depends only on ``n``. Its length ``M``
is a smooth number with ``M \ge 2n - 1``, so the convolution is two transforms of smooth lengths
and one pointwise product. The kernel's transform is computed once when the plan is made.

Per line, Bluestein costs two transforms of length ``M`` and a pointwise product, so it is used
only for the part of a length that the smooth codelets cannot cover. On aarch64 the convolution
length is the smallest 5-smooth number with ``M \ge 2n - 1``. On x86 a cost model fitted to
measured chains picks the fastest 7-smooth length in ``[2n - 1,\, 1.4\,(2n - 1)]``: a length with
many radix 9, 5 or 3 stages transforms several times slower than a power of two a few percent
larger (`src/plan.jl`).

On x86 a convolution of 8 MiB or more (``M \ge 2^{19}`` in `Float64`) runs as a
[four-step](@ref "Long one-dimensional transforms: four-step") transform on the calling thread
instead of a chain. A chain of that length no longer fits in cache and makes one pass over
memory per stage; the four-step makes two. On a Zen 2 core this is 1.06× faster at ``2^{19}``
and 1.3× from ``2^{20}`` on. On the M1 the chain is faster at every length measured (up to
``2^{22}``), so there the chains stay.

### Lengths with a smooth part

A length ``n = s\,q`` whose large prime factors make up ``q`` and whose small ones make up
``s > 1`` does not need a convolution of length ``2n``. Since ``s`` and ``q`` are coprime, one
Cooley–Tukey step with ``j = j_2 + q\,j_1`` and ``k = k_1 + s\,k_2`` splits the transform:

```math
X_{k_1 + s k_2} = \sum_{j_2=0}^{q-1} e^{\sigma 2\pi i\, j_2 k_2/q}\;
  e^{\sigma 2\pi i\, j_2 k_1/n} \sum_{j_1=0}^{s-1} x_{j_2 + q j_1}\, e^{\sigma 2\pi i\, j_1 k_1/s}.
```

1. A chain of length ``s`` over the ``q`` interleaved lines ``j_2`` (the inner sum),
2. multiplication by the twiddle factors ``e^{\sigma 2\pi i\, j_2 k_1/n}``, folded into the first
   chirp product of the next step,
3. ``s`` Bluestein transforms of length ``q`` (the outer sum), each written with stride ``s``.

The arithmetic is about the same as one Bluestein transform of length ``n``: ``s`` convolutions
of length about ``2q`` are again about ``2n`` points. The gain is where the data lives. A
convolution of length ``2q`` fits in cache, one of length ``2n`` often does not. On a Zen 2 core
(one thread, `Float64`) ``262145 = 65 \cdot 4033`` takes 6.6 ms instead of 16.8 ms, and
``1048577 = 17 \cdot 61681`` takes 43 ms instead of 89 ms.

When ``q`` itself is large it is split again: ``q`` becomes the full power of its largest prime,
and the first step of length ``s = n/q`` is again a split transform (or a Bluestein transform
when ``s`` has no smooth part). ``3660001 = 73 \cdot 181 \cdot 277`` runs as Bluestein transforms of
length 13213 and 277. Each split costs one more pass over memory, so a leftover ``q`` stays one
Bluestein transform as long as its convolution takes at most 4 MiB. Below that, splitting
measured 1.15–1.5× slower; above it, 1.02–1.4× faster (`composite_split` in `src/plan.jl`).

Rader's algorithm, which turns a prime length ``p`` into a cyclic convolution of length
``p - 1``, would halve the convolution where ``p - 1`` is smooth. Of 45 random lengths with a
large prime factor between ``2^{10}`` and ``2^{22}``, five qualify; FFTL does not implement it.

## Multidimensional transforms without transposition

A transform along dimension ``d`` of an array with strides ``S_d`` is a set of lines with stride
``S_d``. FFTL does not copy the data into lines. The ``S_d`` adjacent lines of a dimension with stride
``S_d`` are processed together, one SIMD lane per line, so no transposition is needed. Dimensions
1 and 2 are fused per plane when the plane fits in cache.

## Long one-dimensional transforms: four-step

A single long line with a smooth length ``n = n_1 n_2`` on several threads uses the four-step
algorithm:

1. ``n_2`` transforms of length ``n_1`` over the columns,
2. multiplication by the twiddle factors ``e^{\sigma 2\pi i\, j_1 k_2/n}``,
3. ``n_1`` transforms of length ``n_2`` over the rows.

Each of the two transform steps is split over the threads, with a barrier in between. The
factorisation ``n_1 n_2`` is chosen when the plan is made (`fourstep_split`).

## Real transforms

For even ``n = 2M``, a real line is read as ``M`` complex values
``z_j = x_{2j} + i\,x_{2j+1}``, which is the memory layout as it is. One complex DFT of length
``M`` then gives the half spectrum:

```math
X_k = \tfrac12\bigl(Z_k + \overline{Z_{M-k}}\bigr) - \tfrac{i}{2}\, e^{-2\pi i k/n}\bigl(Z_k - \overline{Z_{M-k}}\bigr).
```

The pairs ``k`` and ``M-k`` share their products, so the work is half of a complex transform of
length ``n``. Odd lengths use a complex DFT of length ``n`` directly. The inverse (`c2r`) runs
the same steps backwards.

## Real-to-real transforms

Each kind is computed from a real or complex DFT of a rearranged input. Examples:

- **DCT-II.** The even-indexed inputs go forward and the odd-indexed ones backward:
  ``v = (x_0, x_2, x_4, \dots, x_5, x_3, x_1)``. A real DFT of ``v``, multiplied by
  ``e^{-i\pi k/(2n)}``, gives the DCT-II. This is the reordering that makes the DCT cost one
  real DFT of length ``n``.
- **DST-II and DST-IV** are DCTs of the input with alternating signs, read in reverse order.
- **DCT-I and DST-I** extend the input to an even or odd sequence of length ``2(n-1)`` or
  ``2(n+1)`` and take one real DFT of that length.
- **DHT** is the real DFT with the real and imaginary parts combined: ``\operatorname{Re} F_k - \operatorname{Im} F_k``.

The formulas are in the header comment of `src/r2r.jl`. Their inverses and scale factors are
listed on [the cosine, sine and Hartley page](@ref "Cosine, sine and Hartley transforms").

## Passes

`plan_pass` applies one 1D transform to all lines of a strided array with the same kernel. It
lets a library assemble its own multidimensional transform one dimension at a time. NFFT.jl
transforms its oversampled grid this way; it skips the boxes of the grid that it does not need
itself, so the passes themselves carry no pruning.
